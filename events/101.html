WOAH calls on its Members to strengthen surveillance, early warning and preparedness for vector-borne diseases (VBDs). A vector-borne disease is a disease that can be transmitted from one animal or person to another through a living organism, known as a vector. Vectors are most often insects, such as mosquitoes, ticks, fleas or sandflies, which can carry and transmit pathogens. Analysis of official reports submitted through the World Animal Health Information System (WAHIS) shows a steady increase of the frequency (incidence) of some of these VDBs and the overall number of countries reporting these diseases. Vector-borne diseases are currently showing a marked change in global distribution, with climate change acting as an important driver, alongside anthropogenic factors, vectors ecology, ecological disruption, higher mobility of animals and humans. Analysis of official information reported through WAHIS indicates a marked increase in reported exceptional epidemiological events for several VBDs with important implications for animal health, biodiversity conservation, food security, livelihoods and the resilience of Veterinary Services worldwide. A marked rise since 2023 Data reported through WAHIS reveal a marked rise in VBDs notified to WOAH through WAHIS from 2023 onward, concentrated in a small number of diseases and countries. Bluetongue accounts for the largest amount of this increase, with an average of 38 exceptional epidemiological events per year recorded in 2023–2025 compared with an average of 9.1 events per year between 2015 and 2022 — driven mainly by the incursion of the BTV-3 serotype into north-western Europe beginning in autumn 2023. West Nile fever (a zoonosis that can cause encephalitis in humans) events increased substantially from 8.0 per year on average to 55 per year. Equine infectious anemia showed more moderate increase, led by an increase in reporting of cases in countries in the Americas. By contrast, other listed vector-borne diseases, including lumpy skin disease , have generally remained close to their historical baseline in term of number of exceptional epidemiological events, but have significantly increased their geographic range from Africa to Middle East, Europe and Asia. Since 2005, bluetongue alone has accounted for the majority of all VBD outbreaks reported to WOAH, with lumpy skin disease the next most frequently reported. Regional patterns vary considerably. Since 2022, Europe has reported the overwhelming majority of bluetongue outbreaks, while Africa has remained the primary region affected by Rift Valley fever, and the Americas have accounted for the majority of equine infectious anemia and western equine encephalitis cases. These data should be interpreted as trends in reported events , rather than as a direct measure of changes in disease incidence. Changes in surveillance intensity, diagnostic capacity and notification practices can influence reporting alongside genuine changes in disease occurrence and distribution. Climate change as a structural driver The observed trends are consistent with the demonstrated relationship between climate change and vector ecology highlighted in WOAH’s 2025 State of the World’s Animal Health report . Rising temperatures, changing rainfall patterns and land-use modifications are altering vector density, activity periods, survival and geographical range. In some areas, conditions may allow vectors to reproduce throughout the year, substantially extending their activity period and increasing opportunities for pathogen transmission. These changes can create conditions favourable to the establishment or transmission of VBDs into previously unaffected, often temperate, regions. WOAH reporting has documented changes in the geographical distribution of several VBDs, including an increase in the maximum latitude at which outbreaks have been reported. Other factors—including ecological change, unregulated animal movements and trade, and changes in wildlife–livestock–human interactions—can also influence VBD emergence and spread. Many of the vectors involved pathogens that affect both animals and humans, reinforcing the need for surveillance and response that spans the animal–human–environment interface. Some vector-borne diseases involve complex interactions between animals, humans, vectors and the environment. Effective prevention and control require an integrated One Health approach involving Veterinary Services, public health authorities, environmental agencies, research institutions and other relevant stakeholders. Control options may be limited, with vaccination being an effective measure where available. Other preventive measures include reducing vector breeding sites, using mechanical protection and, where appropriate, repellents. Environmental insecticide use should be carefully considered due to potential impacts on non-target organisms and ecosystems. Continued research is also needed to develop and evaluate biological control approaches. WOAH’s international standards — including the Terrestrial Code recommendations on surveillance for arthropod vectors — provide the scientific foundation for Members to assess vector presence, distribution and abundance, and to design surveillance strategies proportionate to risk. WOAH also supports its Members through innovative early-warning approaches. Since 2022, the PROVNA project has worked with Veterinary Services in North Africa to combine earth observation and climate data with disease occurrence data reported through WAHIS. The project is building models capable of identifying areas with environmental and climatic characteristics suitable for VBDs presence and transmission. Such approaches demonstrate how transdisciplinary collaboration between epidemiologists, entomologists, data scientists and Veterinary Services can shift disease response from reactive to anticipatory. WOAH calls on its Members to: Strengthen integrated surveillance and early warning , combining vector, disease and environmental data to detect changes in distribution, abundance and transmission risk at an early stage before they escalate; Report vector-borne disease events promptly and transparently through the World Animal Health Information System (WAHIS), including relevant information on serotypes and strains where available; Invest in entomological, laboratory and diagnostic capacity , including within national Veterinary Services and in collaboration with WOAH Reference Centres, to sustain vector monitoring and disease surveillance over the long term; Adopt risk-based, science-based control measures , including timely vaccination with quality-assured and epidemiologically appropriate vaccines, vector control and movement management, adapted to the local ecology and epidemiological situation; Strengthen cross-sectoral and cross-border collaboration , including through regional networks and initiatives such as PROVNA, GF-TADs, VectorNet, MediLabSecure among others and the Quadripartite One Health activities, to anticipate and respond to vectors that do not recognise national boundaries; and Facilitate safe trade through science-based measures , ensuring that import risk management measures are proportionate to the identified risk and consistent with WOAH International Standards. As several factors mentioned above continue to reshape the ecology of disease vectors, investment in surveillance, laboratory capacity and Veterinary Services is essential not only for animal health but also for food security, livelihood, biodiversity protection, as well as global health security. WOAH remains committed to supporting its Members through its international standards, expert networks, data systems and scientific collaboration to anticipate, prevent and respond to vector-borne disease evolving threat.
